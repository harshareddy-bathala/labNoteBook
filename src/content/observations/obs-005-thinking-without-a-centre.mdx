Most of the AI I read about assumes a centre. One model sees everything, decides, and
issues commands. A plant has no such thing. It has no brain, no central unit, and it
still defends itself against a grazing animal, steers towards light and spreads its
roots. PhytoNet started as a question about that.

## The question

If a plant's body is a graph of tissues and I let the tissues learn how to talk to
each other, do they invent something like real plant signalling, or something new?

## The setup

Each tissue reads its local environment, exchanges signals with its neighbours and
acts: grow, thicken, steer or defend. Reward comes from survival, biomass and
reproduction.

The headline controller has three deliberate constraints:

- **Decentralised.** Every tissue runs the same rule. There is no coordinator.
- **Interpretable.** The rule is small enough to read, not a network of weights.
- **Electrical.** Tissues talk through membrane potentials, action potentials and
  systemic wound signals, as a real plant does.

The rule is evolved with the cross-entropy method, with no backpropagation. For
comparison I kept two neural controllers as baselines: a PPO policy and a graph
network with learnable messages between tissues.

## What happened

Across multiple seeds and with statistical tests, the brain-free controller beat the
neural baselines overall. The gap was largest under herbivory, a scenario with
grazing damage, where it came out ahead by 2.83 (p = 0.008). The wound-to-defence
electrical response is what pays off there.

The learned signalling also showed three signatures that real plants show:

1. **Systemic spread.** A local wound produces a response far from the wound.
2. **A slow, finite-speed wave.** The signal travels, it does not teleport.
3. **A shoot-directional response.** The reaction is directed along the shoot.

## The finding I trust most

Signalling helped for **coordination problems** such as defence. It did not help with
plain environmental variability. When the challenge is only that conditions change,
a tissue can respond to its own local reading. When the challenge is that the right
action for one part depends on what is happening somewhere else, something has to
travel between the parts.

That is a narrow claim and I would rather keep it narrow. It does not say a
decentralised rule beats a learned policy in general. It says where communication
earned its cost in this setting, and where it did not.

## Why it matters to me

Thinking of intelligence as one big model deciding for everything is a design choice,
not a necessity. A system of simple parts, each acting on local information and a
cheap shared signal, can handle problems that need coordination. It is also easier to
inspect, because the rule is one thing I can read.
