Before I wrote a single line of code for AcadMetrics, I spent three weeks
doing nothing but talking to students, sketching data models on graph paper,
and reading university transcripts from four different institutions.

## Understanding the Domain

Academic data is deceptively simple on the surface. Courses, grades, semesters.
But look closer and edge cases appear everywhere: credit-hour variations,
pass/fail courses that don't affect GPA, transfer credits with different
grading scales, repeated courses that sometimes replace and sometimes average.

Every university encodes these differently. There is no standard.

## Talking to Users First

I interviewed twelve students across three universities. The questions were
open-ended: how they track progress, what they wish they could understand faster.

> _"Nobody wanted more data. Everyone wanted fewer numbers and more meaning.?"_

## Modeling Before Coding

Armed with interview data, I started modeling in TypeScript before touching
infrastructure.
```typescript
type GradeValue =
	| { kind: 'letter'; value: 'A' | 'B' | 'C' | 'D' | 'F' }
	| { kind: 'numeric'; value: number; scale: number }
	| { kind: 'passfail'; passed: boolean };
```

The discriminated union became the breakthrough. Instead of flattening grades
into a scalar, the model preserved semantic meaning.

## What the Data Taught Me

Data modeling is product design. The shape of your schema determines what
questions your product can answer.

Three weeks of research before coding felt slow at the time. In retrospect,
it was the fastest path to a working product.
